# Viatris CTIC Program Design and Enterprise Threat Modeling

## Project Summary
A three-part CTI and threat-modeling program was designed for a global pharmaceutical enterprise (Viatris), moving from attack surface discovery to adversary modeling and finally to a deployable Cyber Threat Intelligence Capability (CTIC) and fusion-center operating model. The work was structured to produce artifacts a security org can actually run: scoped intelligence requirements, prioritized risks, realistic attack paths, and an actionable monitoring and reporting plan spanning cloud, enterprise IT, and manufacturing-adjacent environments.

## Stage 1: OSINT Recon and Attack Surface Mapping
The external and organizational attack surface was enumerated using OSINT and structured reconnaissance. Public signals (job postings, vendor/tooling references, technology stack hints, and role descriptions) were used to infer likely enterprise systems and security control maturity, then translated into concrete exposure hypotheses (where credential theft would land, which admin planes matter, and where vulnerable legacy dependencies could exist). The output of this stage was an attack-surface view that ties assets to likely entry points, identifies vulnerability discovery channels (including scraping job postings and public tech signals for stack weaknesses), and sets up “what should be collected” for intelligence rather than collecting everything.

## Stage 2: Threat Actor Profiling and Attack Path Modeling
Adversary classes were profiled (e.g., financially motivated actors, IP theft targeting pharma R&D, insider threat, and coercion-style threats) and mapped to motivations, means, and likely objectives. Threat modeling language was applied to turn narratives into testable attack paths: assets were paired with access vectors, preconditions, and outcomes, then expanded into attack trees that show how initial access could translate into privilege escalation, lateral movement, and exfiltration. Social engineering was treated as a first-class vector by drafting phishing email flows from reconnaissance-derived context, explicitly connecting “what the attacker would know” to “what a convincing lure would look like” and where controls should intercept it.

## Stage 3: CTIC Architecture, Collection Plan, and Operational Outputs
A CTIC and fusion-center plan was produced that translates the above into operations: priority intelligence requirements (PIRs), collection sources (OSINT + internal telemetry), and a reporting cadence aligned to stakeholders (SOC, IR, GRC, leadership). Tooling and telemetry were treated as an end-to-end pipeline (collection → enrichment → analysis → dissemination), with clear guidance on where detections and intelligence outputs should live (SIEM/SOAR, case management, and executive reporting). The outcome is a cohesive program blueprint that connects recon to modeling to measurable security work: what to monitor, why it matters, how to escalate, and how to communicate risk in a way that supports real decisions.
